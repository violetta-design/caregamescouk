Dealing with Repeated Questions in Dementia: Why It Happens & How to Respond

"Where's mum?" "What time is it?" "Are we going home soon?" — asked five times in ten minutes. If you're caring for someone with dementia, repeated questions are one of the most common, and most exhausting, parts of daily life. It isn't a habit they can control, and understanding why it happens can make it much easier to respond with patience each time.

Why Do They Keep Asking the Same Question?

Dementia affects short-term memory first, which means the answer you gave thirty seconds ago may already be gone. The question isn't being asked to annoy you; it's being asked for the first time, every time, from their perspective. Often the question itself is a sign of an underlying feeling, such as anxiety, boredom, or a need for reassurance, rather than a genuine need for the specific information requested.

What Usually Helps

  • Answer as if it's the first time. Sighing or saying "I already told you" can cause confusion or distress, since they have no memory of being told.
  • Keep the answer short and calm. A brief, reassuring reply is easier to take in than a long explanation.
  • Look for the feeling behind the question. "Where's mum?" may really mean "I feel unsettled" — a hand to hold or a reassuring tone can help more than the factual answer.
  • Use written reminders. A large, visible clock, calendar, or note on the door can sometimes reduce how often a question needs to be asked aloud.
  • Redirect gently. After answering, shift into an activity or topic they enjoy, which can ease the anxious loop.
  • Step away briefly if you need to. If you feel frustration building, a short pause in another room is better for both of you than a sharp response.

When the Question Is About a Deceased Loved One

Repeated questions about a parent, spouse, or friend who has died are especially difficult. Correcting the fact directly ("Mum passed away years ago") can cause the person to relive the loss and grieve again, every single time. Many carers find it kinder to gently redirect: "Tell me about your mum" invites a warm memory instead of a painful correction, and often settles the anxiety just as effectively.

Looking After Yourself Too

Repeated questions can wear down even the most patient carer, especially late in the day. It's not a sign of failure to feel frustrated — it's a completely normal response to a genuinely repetitive, tiring situation. Short breaks, a support network, and realistic expectations for yourself all matter as much as any technique.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it okay to feel frustrated when they ask the same thing again?
Yes, completely. Frustration is a normal human response to repetition, not a reflection of how much you care. What matters is how you manage that feeling in the moment, not whether you feel it at all.

Should I remind them they've already asked?
Generally, it's kinder not to, since they have no memory of asking. A calm, fresh answer each time tends to cause far less distress than a correction.

Will this get better or worse over time?
Repetition often changes as dementia progresses, sometimes becoming more frequent, sometimes shifting to different topics entirely. It generally isn't something that resolves with reminders or reasoning, so adapting your response is usually more effective than trying to stop the behaviour itself.
