Understanding Dementia Wandering: Why It Happens and What Helps

Quick answer: Dementia wandering usually happens for a reason — confusion about time or place, a wish to go somewhere familiar from the past, restlessness, or an unmet need like hunger, pain, or needing the toilet. It isn't random, even when it looks that way. Reducing risk means combining practical safety measures (door alarms, secure locks placed out of the eyeline, identification jewellery) with addressing the underlying need before restlessness builds, such as offering a walk, a snack, or simple reassurance.

Why it happens

Wandering is one of the most worrying behaviours for carers, but it almost always has a cause behind it. Someone may be trying to get to a former home, a old workplace, or to collect children from school — places that feel urgently real to them, even decades later. Others wander simply because they're restless, bored, or their body is telling them something (they're hungry, in pain, or need the toilet) and they don't have the words to say so.

Understanding wandering as communication, rather than as "bad behaviour," changes how carers respond to it — with curiosity and reassurance rather than frustration.

Practical ways to reduce risk

  • Fit door alarms or sensors that alert you when an exterior door opens
  • Place locks slightly higher or lower than eye level, where they're less likely to be noticed
  • Provide a safe, enclosed space to walk freely, like a secure garden
  • Use identification jewellery, a card in a pocket, or a GPS tracker if appropriate
  • Keep a recent photo and physical description easily accessible
  • Notice patterns — does wandering happen at a certain time of day, like late afternoon?
  • Offer a walk, snack, or drink before restlessness builds, especially in the late afternoon
  • Reduce clutter and confusing signage near exits that might prompt "escape" thinking
  • Let trusted neighbours know, so they can call you if they see your loved one out alone

A moment from real life

One of the people I've cared for used to stand by the front door most afternoons, insisting she needed to "get back for the children." Rather than correcting her, I learned to say, "The children are looked after today, why don't we have a cup of tea first?" Nine times out of ten, that gentle redirection and a few minutes of connection was enough. The urge to leave rarely came from wanting to escape — it came from worry, and worry responds far better to reassurance than to argument.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Arguing or correcting: Telling someone "you don't need to collect the children, they're grown up" usually increases distress rather than resolving it.
  • Ignoring the pattern: If wandering happens at a similar time each day, that's valuable information — use it to plan ahead.
  • Relying on locks alone: Physical security matters, but addressing the emotional need behind the wandering prevents far more incidents.
  • Waiting too long to act if someone goes missing: Search familiar routes immediately, and don't hesitate to contact the police if they aren't found quickly.

Frequently asked questions

Why does someone with dementia wander?
Wandering is often driven by confusion about time or place, a wish to go somewhere from the past like a former home or job, restlessness, boredom, or an unmet need such as hunger, pain, or needing the toilet. It is rarely random — there is usually a reason behind it, even if it isn't obvious at first.
What time of day does dementia wandering usually happen?
Wandering often increases in the late afternoon and evening, linked to sundowning, but it can happen at any time, including at night when someone wakes disoriented.
How can I stop someone with dementia from wandering off?
You can't always stop the urge to wander, but you can reduce risk with door alarms or sensors, secure locks placed out of the eyeline, a safe enclosed space to walk in, identification jewellery or cards, and by addressing the underlying need, such as offering a walk, snack, or reassurance before restlessness builds.
Is wandering dangerous?
Yes, wandering can be dangerous if someone leaves a safe environment and becomes lost, especially in poor weather, near roads, or without appropriate clothing. This is why prevention and quick response planning both matter.
What should I do if someone with dementia goes missing?
Search the immediate area and likely familiar routes first, such as a former home, workplace, or place of worship. Contact the police promptly if they are not found quickly, as time matters. Keep a recent photo and description ready in advance for this situation.
Does wandering mean dementia is getting worse?
Not necessarily. Wandering can appear at different stages and doesn't always signal significant progression on its own. It is more often linked to specific triggers like restlessness, disorientation, or an unmet need than to overall disease severity.
