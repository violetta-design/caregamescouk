Conversation Starters for Dementia: What to Say When Words Get Harder

Quick answer: The best conversation starters for dementia focus on feelings and long-term memories rather than facts or recent events. Instead of asking "Do you remember...?", try open, sensory prompts like "Tell me about your wedding day" or looking through old photos together and asking "Who's this?" Questions about childhood, family, favourite foods, music, or places often stay accessible long after short-term memory fades, because emotional and long-term memories are typically preserved much longer in dementia. The goal isn't a correct answer — it's connection.

Why it works

Dementia affects short-term memory first, but emotional memory and memories from early adulthood tend to stay intact much longer. That's why a question like "What did you have for breakfast?" can draw a blank, while "What was your mother's kitchen like?" can bring a warm, detailed story.

Open-ended, feeling-based questions also remove the pressure of a "right answer." There's no test to fail, which means less anxiety and more genuine conversation — for both of you.

Practical conversation starters to try

  • "Tell me about the house you grew up in."
  • "What was your first job like?"
  • "Who's this in the photo?" (while looking through old pictures together)
  • "What was your wedding day like?"
  • "Did you have a pet growing up?"
  • "What was your favourite meal your mum used to cook?"
  • "What music did you love when you were young?"
  • "Tell me about your best friend growing up."
  • "What was your favourite place to go on holiday?"
  • "What did you want to be when you were little?"
  • "What was school like for you?"
  • "Do you have a favourite flower or smell that reminds you of somewhere?"
  • "Tell me about your brothers and sisters."
  • "What was your wedding dress like?" / "What did you wear to your wedding?"
  • "What's a song that always makes you want to dance?"

A moment from real life

One of the people I care for lost her sight some years ago, but she lights up the moment I mention the sea. She'll talk for twenty minutes about a childhood holiday, the smell of the water, a song that was playing on the radio that summer. She couldn't tell you what she had for lunch — but she remembers every detail of that beach. That's the moment I stopped asking "do you remember" and started asking "tell me about."

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Testing memory: "Do you remember me?" can cause real distress if the answer is no. Introduce yourself gently instead.
  • Correcting facts: If they say something factually wrong, it's usually kinder to go along with the emotional truth of what they're saying than to correct them.
  • Rushing the pace: Give extra time for a response. Silence isn't a sign to move on.
  • Asking multiple questions at once: One simple, open question at a time works best.
  • Talking about them, not to them: Even with more advanced dementia, include the person directly rather than discussing them in the room.

Frequently asked questions

What should you not say to someone with dementia?
Avoid quiz-style questions like "Do you remember...?" or correcting them when they get facts wrong. These can cause embarrassment or distress. Instead, focus on feelings and open, low-pressure prompts.
How do you talk to someone with dementia who doesn't recognise you?
Introduce yourself warmly without testing them, stay calm and reassuring, and focus on the feeling of connection rather than whether they remember your name. Gentle, familiar topics like music or old photos can help ease the moment.
Why do people with dementia remember the past better than recent events?
Long-term and emotional memories are stored differently in the brain and are typically preserved much longer than short-term memory in dementia, which is why childhood and early-life topics often stay accessible.
What are good conversation topics for dementia patients?
Childhood, family, weddings, first jobs, favourite foods, music, pets, and old photographs are reliably good topics because they connect to long-term and emotional memory.
How long should a conversation with someone with dementia last?
There's no fixed rule. Follow their energy: some days a few minutes of connection is enough, other days they may want to talk much longer. Watch for signs of tiredness or agitation and let them lead the pace.
Can music help start a conversation with someone with dementia?
Yes. Music from someone's teenage or young adult years is strongly linked to emotional memory and can often prompt conversation, singing along, or storytelling even when other memory has faded.
