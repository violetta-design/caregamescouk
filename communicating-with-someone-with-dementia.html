Communicating with Someone with Dementia

Quick answer: Keep it simple, calm, and patient. Use short sentences, one idea at a time, gentle tone of voice, and give them time to respond. Avoid correcting or arguing about facts — connection matters more than being right.

Why communication changes

Dementia affects how the brain processes language — finding words, following fast conversation, and remembering what was just said all become harder. This isn't the person "not trying" or "not listening." It's the disease affecting the tools they'd normally use to communicate.

The good news: connection doesn't disappear. Tone, facial expression, and feeling understood still come through, even when words get harder to find or follow.

Practical ways to communicate better

  • Approach from the front, at eye level, so you don't startle them
  • Use their name and identify yourself if there's any doubt
  • Speak slowly, in short, simple sentences — one thought at a time
  • Ask one question at a time, and wait patiently for the answer
  • Use yes/no questions when possible, rather than open-ended ones
  • Keep your tone warm and calm — tone matters more than words
  • Use gestures, pointing, or objects to support what you're saying
  • Avoid quizzing ("Do you remember who this is?") — it can cause distress
  • If they say something factually wrong, don't correct — respond to the feeling instead
  • Reduce background noise and distractions during conversation
  • Give plenty of time — resist the urge to finish their sentences
  • Use touch (a hand on theirs) if welcome, to add reassurance

"One afternoon, the person I care for kept asking about someone who wasn't there. Instead of explaining, I just said 'Tell me about them' — and her whole face lit up. She didn't need the facts corrected. She needed to feel heard."

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Correcting or arguing about facts, dates, or names
  • Talking over them, or finishing their sentences too quickly
  • Asking multiple questions at once
  • Speaking about them in the third person while they're present
  • Raising your voice, assuming they can't hear (usually it's processing, not hearing)
  • Testing their memory as a way of "checking in"

FAQs

Should I correct them if they say something untrue?

Generally no. Focus on the emotion behind what they're saying rather than the factual accuracy. Gently redirecting works better than correcting.

What if they repeat the same question over and over?

Answer calmly each time, as if it's the first time. Repetition is common and isn't intentional — it's often linked to short-term memory changes.

Is it normal for them to struggle to find words?

Yes, this is one of the most common early signs. Offer a gentle prompt or guess if they seem stuck, but let them lead.

How do I handle it when they get frustrated mid-conversation?

Stay calm, slow down, and simplify. Sometimes it helps to pause the conversation altogether and return to it later.

Does body language really make a difference?

Yes — often more than words. A warm expression, gentle tone, and relaxed posture can communicate safety even when words don't land.

Can games or activities help with communication?

Yes. Structured, low-pressure activities (like memory games) create natural opportunities for conversation without the pressure of "just talking."
